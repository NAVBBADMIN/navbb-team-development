The Trust Gap

They already understand the program. The problem isn't information. The problem is they don't know yet that people like them, with dogs like theirs, have done this and it went fine.

The Concept

When someone says "I'll think about it," the instinct is to give them more information. Another flyer. A follow-up email. A callback in two weeks. And sometimes that works. But more often, the person who said "I'll think about it" already has all the information they need. What they don't have is confidence.

Confidence that other dogs like theirs have done this and it was fine. Confidence that the people in their daycare community — people they actually know and trust — have made this same decision. Confidence that the recruiter standing in front of them isn't the only person who thinks this is a good idea.

That's the trust gap. And closing it doesn't require a better pitch. It requires a different kind of evidence — the kind that comes from someone who isn't you.

A Patricia Moment
Patricia has a growing list of people who said they'd think about it.

Patricia's follow-up email list has 23 people on it. She emails them all every two weeks. She writes the same email to all 23. She has not yet considered that at three of her locations, she sees existing donors in the lobby on the same day as the maybes — people who could say in thirty seconds what Patricia's email never quite manages to convey.

Patricia knows Murphy. Patricia has spoken to Murphy's owner many times. Patricia has never once asked Murphy's owner to say hello to someone who was on the fence. Patricia has never made that connection.

Patricia's list now has 24 people on it.

The Three Closers

When the information isn't the issue, the solution is trust. Here are the three ways to build it fast — each one suited to a different situation in the lobby.

01
The Cheerleader

An existing donor owner, standing in the same lobby, who will tell a hesitant parent "yeah, we've been doing this for a year and a half — it's totally fine." This is the most powerful closer available to you. It costs nothing. The credibility is instant because it comes from a peer, not a salesperson.

Use when: a maybe and a Murphy are in the same lobby at the same time.
02
The Breadcrumb

Getting the daycare staff invested enough in the program that they mention it when you're not there. "Oh, you should talk to our blood bank recruiter — a bunch of dogs here are in the program." Staff who feel ownership of the partnership become part-time recruiters. They plant seeds that are already growing when you walk back in.

Use when: you're building a relationship at a location over time and want to work even when you're not physically there.
03
The Bypass

When the person picking up the dog isn't the one who makes decisions, going directly to the decision-maker. Ask permission to get the email. "Do you mind if I reach out to her directly with the information? That way she has everything she needs in her own inbox." Cut the middleman. Don't wait for a summary that may never arrive.

Use when: you hear "I need to ask my wife" or "let me talk to my husband" and the person in front of you won't be the one deciding.

A maybe sitting in your email list for three weeks isn't a pipeline. It's a stalled conversation that needed a different approach two weeks ago.

What Murphy Actually Does

Murphy isn't a person. Murphy is a concept — the existing donor whose owner, if asked, would happily tell a hesitant parent what it's actually like. Murphy is at every location that has donors. The question is whether the recruiter knows who Murphy is and uses them.

Biscuit's Origin Story
Biscuit almost didn't sign up. Then his owner met someone who was already in the program.

Biscuit's owner was interested but hesitant. She had questions that went beyond the information on the flyer — questions about what it was actually like for her dog, not what the program was like in the abstract. The recruiter couldn't fully answer those questions. Not because she didn't know the program, but because she wasn't a dog owner who had been through it.

What she could do was introduce Biscuit's owner to someone who was. That other dog owner talked to Biscuit's mom for three minutes. She described what the donation process looked like for her dog. She mentioned her dog actually seemed to enjoy the attention. She said she'd been doing it for over a year and had never had a problem.

Biscuit's mom signed him up that day. The recruiter didn't close that one. Murphy did.

On the FOMO Approach
A Different Kind of Cheerleader

There's a version of the cheerleader approach that works at the location level, not just with individual owners. When a daycare finds out that other daycares in their area — ones they consider competitors — are in the program and are proud of it, they become more invested in building their own numbers.

Staff who feel like their location is in the mix, who can say "we've got twelve dogs in the program here," become part-time advocates. They mention it. They remember it when a parent asks about the dog next to theirs in the lobby. They help you without being asked because they feel ownership of the partnership.

Building that investment is slow. But once it's there, it works for you every day you're not there.
