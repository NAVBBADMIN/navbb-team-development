The Thing Nobody Said

~8 min · Click through each scene · One question at the end.
Scene 1 of 6
The Setup

Something is visibly off. Four people notice. Four different choices.

A weekly content piece goes out to the team. Something is missing from it — an interactive element that's supposed to be there. It's not subtle. Anyone who opened it would see the gap.

Four people on the team notice within the first few days. Each of them makes a different choice about what to do with what they noticed. Watch what happens in each version.

Version 1 — The Wait

Patricia notices. Patricia waits.

Patricia's internal reasoning

"It's probably being addressed. Someone else definitely noticed. I don't want to bring it up if it's already being handled. I don't want to seem like I'm creating problems. Maybe I'll wait until the next meeting and see if it comes up."

Patricia waits. Nobody brings it up at the meeting because nobody else brought it up before the meeting, and the meeting moved on. Two weeks pass. The gap stays there. When it finally surfaces, a week of time had passed and the fix is now overdue.

The cost of waiting: A week of a missed piece. A pattern that, if it repeats, quietly signals to the team that gaps are acceptable. Nobody intended this. It happened through inaction, not action.

Version 2 — The Workaround

Recruiter B notices. Recruiter B works around it.

Recruiter B's reasoning

"The piece is missing but the rest of it is there. I'll just do what I can with what's available. Someone else will probably catch it eventually. It's not my job to police the content."

Recruiter B completes what's available. The missing piece gets treated as optional. The standard subtly shifts. Over time, working around gaps becomes normalized — and when something more significant is missing, the same pattern plays out at a higher cost.

The cost of working around: The gap doesn't get fixed, and the behavior of ignoring gaps becomes a habit. Every workaround without a flag is a vote for "this is acceptable."

Version 3 — The Say Something

Recruiter C notices. Recruiter C says something.

Recruiter C's message

"Hey — noticed the interactive piece wasn't included with this week's content. Not sure if it was intentional or got missed, wanted to flag it just in case."

That's the whole message. One sentence. It took thirty seconds. It didn't require certainty about whose fault it was, or whether it was her place to say it, or whether someone else had already flagged it. It was an observation, delivered cleanly, with no accusation attached.

The outcome: The gap gets fixed the same day. Leadership knows to look for it. Nobody's time was wasted on a one-week delay. Recruiter C spent thirty seconds on a message that saved everyone else the cost of the silence.

Version 4 — The Thorough Email

Recruiter D notices. Recruiter D composes a very thorough email.

Recruiter D wants to make sure the framing is exactly right before sending. She drafts the email carefully. She explains the context, provides background, frames the observation diplomatically, and structures the key point so it doesn't come across as a criticism. She adds a closing paragraph expressing continued support for the program.

The email is four paragraphs long. The key point — something is missing — is in paragraph three. Recruiter D sends it on a Friday afternoon. It goes into a busy inbox. It gets flagged for later. It is not read until Monday. The gap remains through the weekend.

The lesson: Good communication is usually simpler than we make it. The instinct to protect the framing is understandable. But a thirty-second flag — even imperfectly worded — almost always outperforms a perfectly crafted email that arrives late and gets buried.

Your Turn

Which version do you recognize in yourself — and what's one thing that makes it easier to be Version 3?

Be honest. Patricia's wait and Recruiter D's thorough email are both understandable impulses. What specifically gets in the way of the thirty-second flag for you — and what would make it easier to just send it?
