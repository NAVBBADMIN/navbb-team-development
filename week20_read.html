Get the Number,
Not the Registration

Every registration sets a chain in motion: a pending line, a screening, a collection plan, a number on the dashboard. A maybe belongs in your notes, not in FETCH.

What a Sign-Up Actually Is

A registration is not a note to self. The moment a donor is registered, it becomes a line on the pending list, a name the medical team may screen, a donor the collection team plans a day around, and a figure on the dashboard that leadership reads on Monday. Each of those steps costs time, travel, or money.

So when a registration is really a maybe, such as "let me talk to my husband" or "I think Mondays might work," it is a bookmark. A bookmark looks exactly like a real donor right up until the day it does not show up.

A bookmark looks like a sign-up on the dashboard. It only looks different on screening day.

A Patricia Moment
Patricia has 47 pending donors and calls it a healthy pipeline.

Patricia registers every parent who says maybe, so she will not forget them. She has not called any of them. Eleven have never been contacted since the day they were entered. One has been pending since February and Patricia does not recognize the name. She is leaving it there in case it becomes important.

Patricia's pending list is the longest in the region. Patricia's show rate is not. Patricia considers these unrelated.

Register When. Capture the Number When.
Register in FETCH when
You have a real yes

The parent has heard the once-a-month commitment and said yes. You know the location and the day. You have their number and their preferred way to be reached. The dog fits the weight, age, and health basics.

Capture the number when
You have a maybe

The parent wants to check with a spouse, is unsure the schedule works, or you need a medical answer first. Take their number, tell them exactly when you will call, call, and register on the yes.

This is the point leadership made to the whole team this week: the system should not become a contact card. If a parent needs to talk it over, that is fine. Get the contact information, make the call, and let the number in FETCH mean something.

The Commitment Has to Be Said Out Loud
1

Say the monthly part plainly

"This is a once-a-month commitment. If that cannot work for you, just tell me and I will take your dog off the list." That second sentence gives the parent a safe way to say no, and a no you hear early is far cheaper than a no that shows up as an empty screening slot.

2

Name the day and the cadence

"We are at the daycare every four weeks on a Monday." A parent who hears the actual day can check the actual calendar. A parent who hears "once a month" may picture something far looser.

3

Check the sign-ups you did not take yourself

When a daycare signs a parent up in your absence, the commitment message often gets lost on the way. This week a donor was registered by the daycare, dad confirmed he would bring him, and nothing happened, even though the daycare said the dog is there several days a week. Ask the daycare to say the commitment out loud, then make your own call to the parent within the week.

4

Archive fast and kindly on a no

A parent registered at Berkeley Creek so the recruiter could reach her. A few days later she said a Monday every month would not work with her family's jobs. The recruiter archived her immediately and said, "If anything changes, please let me know." The list stayed honest and the door stayed open.

Work the Pending List Before It Works You
The pending list is a to-do list, not a trophy case

This week there were 134 pending donors. While going through the list, the medical team found names from April through August with no communication logged at all, plus one donor pending since February with no donations, no notes, and no reason anyone could find for why the donor was still there. That donor was archived on the spot.

The request is simple: anything from April through August with no communication, call or text. Name the location and the date. Ask whether it works. Say plainly, "If not, we are happy to remove you." Where it helps, mention that some daycares offer free daycare on screening day, or that an office appointment is available.

Run three questions on every pending donor older than about sixty days: Is there a recent communication note? Does this parent know the next specific date? Have I offered an alternative? If the answers are no, no, and no, you have found your call for the day. If a real conversation still ends in no, archive and write the reason in the notes, so the next person does not have to wonder why.

The same rule applies to happy visits. Two or three visits is the working range, and they only build anything when they are consistent. A donor seen twice in several months is not getting more comfortable. She is starting over every time. That is a decision point, and it needs a decision.

Trust Records, Not Labels

This week a parent told the team their dog could not come because of heartworm. The vet records said hookworm. A daycare described three dogs as having pneumonia. Pneumonia is a radiographic diagnosis, and the dog's owner later said it was treated as kennel cough, with no x-rays. Before anyone corrected the record, units were pulled as a safeguard, which means incorrect information from owners can cost real product.

When a medical claim comes up, ask for the vet records and pass them to the medical team. Do not repeat a diagnosis word to daycare staff or other parents. By the second or third person it has changed shape, and the owner often decides what the problem is on the strength of the first word they hear.

Always be recruiting. November and December are the second-best months to recruit, and the numbers do not lie. Just make sure the numbers are real.
