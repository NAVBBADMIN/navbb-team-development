The Bridge Between
Retirement and Return

When a dog gets medically retired, the recruiter's relationship with that parent does not end. It changes. Your job is to stay in contact, track what is happening, and know when to ask the right question.

Medical Retirement Is Not Always the End

When the system retires a dog for a medical reason, it is making a clinical decision based on what the data shows right now. Medication in the system. Abnormal bloodwork. A health flag. Those are real reasons to pause donations. They are not necessarily permanent reasons to stop them.

Mo was on allergy medication. That medication cleared. Mo came back. Ripley was on medication that made her ineligible. Her owner asked how to get her back. That medication stopped. Ripley came back. In both cases, the retirement was correct when it happened. And in both cases, the path back existed — it just needed someone to stay in the relationship long enough to find it.

A medically retired dog is not a lost donor. It is a donor whose situation might change. Your job is to still be there when it does.

A Patricia Moment
Patricia has eleven medically retired donors and has not followed up with any of their parents.

Patricia received the retirement notices. Patricia noted them in her tracker. Patricia assumed the parents know their dog cannot donate and did not reach out. Patricia considers this appropriate. Patricia has not asked about the reason for any of them. Three of those dogs have been off their medication for months and their owners have no idea they could potentially come back. Patricia is preparing a group email to all eleven parents about upcoming donation dates. Patricia has not mentioned that any of them might be eligible again.

Your Role vs. the Medical Team's Role
Recruiter
Relationship and communication
Stay in contact with the parent. Understand what the retirement reason was. Track whether things are changing. Ask the reactivation question when it seems appropriate. Gather any vet records the parent can share. Bring it to the medical team.
Medical Team
Clinical eligibility decisions
Review the records. Determine whether the dog can return. Set the conditions for reactivation. Approve or decline the return. The recruiter does not make this call and does not tell the parent the dog can definitely come back — only that you will check.

The recruiter is the bridge. You are not making medical decisions. You are staying in the relationship, gathering information, and bringing it to the people who can make the call. That is exactly what Hannah does when she gets vet records from a parent and sends them to Dr. Galanis with the question: can this dog come back?

How to Stay in the Relationship
1

Keep showing up at the daycare

The parent still brings their dog to daycare. You still visit that daycare. Use those encounters to check in. "How is Cooper doing? How are his allergies?" You are not asking about donation eligibility. You are maintaining the relationship that makes the reactivation conversation possible later.

2

Know the retirement reason

Before you can know whether reactivation is possible, you need to know why the dog was retired. Was it a specific medication? A bloodwork result? An age threshold? Some retirement reasons are permanent. Others are not. Knowing the difference means you are not chasing something that will never happen — and not missing something that could.

3

Listen for the parent to signal a change

Ripley's mom asked how to get her back. That question was the signal. When a parent brings up their dog's health or mentions that a medication has changed or that their dog has been to the vet, that is the moment to ask: "Has anything changed with the condition that had her retired?" Do not push it. But do not miss the signal either.

4

Ask the medical team, not the parent

When you think reactivation might be possible, bring the question to Dr. Galanis. "The parent says the medication has stopped. Can you tell me what you would need to see to consider reactivating this dog?" Then take that answer back to the parent with a specific path: here is what we need, here is who to contact, here is what happens next.

The Stale Sign-Up Problem

Not every "retired" donor is medically retired. Some dogs get automatically removed from active status because of inactivity — they signed up, never showed, and eventually aged out of the pipeline. The parent does not know this happened. They think they signed up and nobody ever called them.

When Tracy approached a woman at Pet Suites Grove City and the woman said she had signed up a while back and never heard anything, that was a stale sign-up who needed to be re-engaged, not medically cleared. The fix was simple: "Great news, we are starting in two weeks." The woman got excited. That parent was not lost. She was just waiting for someone to show up.

Both situations — the medically retired dog and the stale sign-up — share the same root problem: the relationship went quiet. The fix in both cases is the same too: you show up, you find out what is going on, and you give them a path back.
