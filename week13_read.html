The Conversation
That Keeps Working

You left the lobby. The conversation didn't. Some of the best sign-ups happen weeks or months after your first pitch — not because the person forgot, but because they didn't.

The Delay Isn't Silence

When someone says they're interested and then goes quiet for six weeks, it's easy to read that as a no. It rarely is. People have lives. They leave the daycare pickup and immediately take a phone call. They mean to register and then their kid has a school thing and then it's Saturday and then it's somehow three weeks later. The flyer is on the counter. They think about it sometimes. They just haven't done it yet.

And then something happens. They see a commercial. Their neighbor's dog gets sick. Their own dog has a vet visit and the vet mentions blood donors. Something reconnects them to the conversation you had in the lobby — the conversation they actually remembered — and they sign up.

A delayed sign-up isn't a coincidence. It's proof the original conversation worked. The person remembered you. They remembered what you said. It just took a trigger to move them from "I'll do that someday" to "I'll do that now."

A Patricia Moment
Patricia had a parent in her "Gave Up" column sign up last Tuesday.

Patricia followed up once after the original conversation. She got no response. She moved the parent to her "Gave Up" column. She has 47 people in her "Gave Up" column. She does not know any of their names anymore.

Last Tuesday one of them signed up. Patricia was excited. Patricia posted about it in the team chat. Patricia does not know what she said in the original conversation that made it stick. Patricia does not know what triggered the person to finally register. Patricia cannot replicate it. Patricia considers this a win.

It was a win. The problem is Patricia has 46 other people in that column and no way to build on what actually worked.

What Makes a Conversation Stick

The conversations that produce delayed sign-ups have something in common: the person left feeling something, not just knowing something. They knew the program exists, sure. But more than that, they felt the urgency. They pictured their dog. They thought about what it would mean if their dog ever needed a transfusion. The recruiter made it real for a moment, and that moment stayed with them even after they walked away.

You can't follow up your way to a delayed sign-up. You can only have a good enough first conversation that the sign-up follows without you.

Slow Week vs. Broken Approach
Slow Week
The pipeline is filling

Conversations happened. People were interested. Nothing converted yet. This is normal. The gap between the conversation and the sign-up is real and it varies by person. A slow week on the board doesn't mean a slow week in the pipeline. It might mean a busy week three months from now.

Broken Approach
The conversations aren't landing

People are leaving the conversation without having felt anything. No urgency, no personal connection, no moment that would make the flyer on the counter seem important two weeks later. If you've been having a lot of conversations and getting very few delayed sign-ups, that's the signal to examine the conversation itself — not add more locations.

Biscuit's Owner — The Timeline
Three Weeks Later
She didn't sign up the day she was approached. She signed up the day after she saw the commercial.

The recruiter talked to Biscuit's owner in the daycare lobby on a Tuesday. It was a good conversation. Biscuit's owner said she was definitely interested. She left without signing up because she needed to check with her husband first.

Three weeks passed. The recruiter kept coming to that daycare. She didn't chase Biscuit's owner specifically — she just kept showing up and having good conversations with whoever was there.

Then Biscuit's owner saw something on TV about a dog that needed a blood transfusion and didn't get it in time. She went to her phone, found the program's website, and registered that night. The original conversation is what she was returning to. The recruiter just had to still be there when she came back.

Persistence vs. Chasing
Knowing the Difference

Persistence means you keep showing up at a location and having good conversations with whoever is there. You trust that the pipeline is filling even when the board is quiet. You don't adjust your approach based on one slow week. You keep going.

Chasing means you keep following up with a specific person who has stopped responding. One follow-up after a promising conversation is persistence. Three follow-ups to someone who hasn't replied is chasing. Chasing doesn't produce delayed sign-ups — it produces people who feel hassled and remember you for the wrong reason.

The distinction matters because the feeling they take away from your follow-up is the same feeling they'll have when something triggers them to reconsider. Make sure it's the right feeling.

Bring This To Our Next Meeting
One delayed sign-up — and what you think made it stick

Think of someone who signed up weeks or months after your first conversation. What did you say in that conversation? What do you think triggered them to finally register? If you can't name one, think about a conversation you had recently that felt promising — and what you'd want someone to remember from it three weeks from now.
