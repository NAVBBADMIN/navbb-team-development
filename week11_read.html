Say Something

The cost of silence is almost always higher than the cost of the conversation that didn't happen. The question isn't whether something should be said. It's whether you'll be the one to say it.

Why This Is This Week's Topic

The interactive piece for Week 10 was missed. It wasn't there for two weeks before anyone said anything. That's not a story about blame — leadership can miss things, and does. This one is on that. But the two weeks of silence that followed is worth examining together.

Every person who noticed and waited was doing something that feels natural: not wanting to overstep, not being sure if it was their place, assuming someone else had it handled. All reasonable instincts. All of them, in this case, produced a two-week gap.

Good team communication doesn't wait for the right moment. It happens when something is noticed. That's what this week is about.

Three Directions — Three Different Skills

Communication on a team isn't one thing. It moves in different directions, and each one requires something slightly different from you.

Direction 1 — Upward
Raising something to leadership

The most common version of this — bringing something to your manager's attention — is also the one people are most afraid of getting wrong. The concern is usually that it will sound like a complaint, like you're creating problems instead of solving them, or like you're putting your manager on the spot.

The framing that makes it work: lead with the observation, not the problem. "I noticed X" is neutral. "X is a problem" is a verdict. You're providing information, not making a case. When you frame it as something you noticed, you give leadership the room to respond without feeling like they're being put on trial.

Instead of: "The interactive piece for Week 10 wasn't there, which was a problem."
Try: "Hey, I noticed the interactive piece wasn't included with Week 10 — wanted to flag it in case it was missed."
Direction 2 — Lateral
Raising something with a peer

Peer-to-peer communication is where the most energy is lost on most teams. Something one person does affects another person's work. The person affected says nothing because they don't want the friction. They route around it, work harder than they need to, or carry mild resentment that quietly builds. None of those options are better than the thirty-second conversation that could fix it.

This week's meeting had a good example of it. Donna had a staff member at Dogtopia Charlottesville who kept redirecting conversations away from her pitch. Hannah's advice was direct: go talk to her. Tell her what you need, specifically. Give her the chance to adjust before you assume she won't. That's what lateral communication looks like when it works.

The rule: If something a peer is doing affects your ability to do your job, the right move is to say something to the peer — not to work around it and not to wait until it's a bigger issue.
Direction 3 — Sideways
Raising something you noticed that isn't yours

This is the hardest one. When something is missing, wrong, or unclear and it isn't your direct responsibility — do you say something? Most people don't, for the same reasons. Not my lane. Someone else probably noticed. I don't want to seem like I'm monitoring things that aren't mine to monitor.

But this is exactly the behavior that defines high-functioning teams. The person who says "I noticed this, wanted to flag it" when it isn't their job to notice is taking active ownership of the team's outcomes — not just their own. It costs almost nothing. It often saves a lot.

The question to ask yourself: If I noticed a daycare partner's location had a problem and it wasn't technically my territory, would I mention it to someone? Almost certainly yes. Apply the same standard to your own team.

Silence isn't neutral. When you notice something and don't say it, you've made a choice. The question is whether it's the choice you'd make if you thought about it.

The Chippendale Principle
Communication Isn't Only Verbal

There was a medical equipment vendor who showed up every month at a veterinary hospital he was never going to sell to. He brought food for the staff every time. He was cheerful. He never pushed. And when he didn't show up one month, the entire staff noticed and asked where he was.

He hadn't sold them anything. He wasn't their vendor. But his consistent presence communicated something: I'm invested in this relationship regardless of what I get from it. Years later, the people who worked there still remember him by name.

This is what consistent, proactive presence communicates to a team. The recruiter who checks in when things are going fine — not just when something's wrong — builds the kind of trust that makes raising a problem feel easy when a problem actually exists. Communication culture is built in the ordinary moments, not just the urgent ones.
