Knowing a location and knowing the people in it are two completely different things. One produces visits. The other produces calls on a Tuesday afternoon.

The Rule

Seven years of running a canine blood bank. Seven years of trying every approach. The conclusion that has never changed: one relationship with every donor owner is the only thing that works. Not events. Not volume. Not a perfectly worded email. The relationship.

This isn't a philosophical preference. It's the operational reality of a program where the same people need to keep showing up month after month. A sign-up you got because you had a genuine conversation with someone produces a consistent donor. A sign-up you got because you were in the lobby at the right time produces a dog that shows up twice and disappears. The relationship is what turns a registration into a donor who's still in the program two years from now.

And it starts with one person. Then another. Then another. Not twelve locations at once. One person at a time, across every location you have.

A Patricia Moment
Patricia knows all seventeen of her locations.

Patricia has visited Barkability eleven times. Patricia knows the layout of the lobby. Patricia knows what day is busiest. Patricia knows that the manager's name is Jennifer.

Patricia cannot name a single parent at Barkability who would recognize her name if Jennifer mentioned it. Patricia has had nice conversations with parents at Barkability. Patricia does not remember their names. Patricia does not follow up with them specifically. Patricia visits the location, not the people.

Jennifer has never texted Patricia about a new dog that just joined the daycare. Jennifer has never flagged a parent for Patricia before a visit. Jennifer waves when Patricia arrives and goes back to her desk. Patricia considers this a solid partnership.

Knowing a Location vs. Knowing the People
Knowing a Location
You know the place

You know the layout, the busy days, the manager's name, how long you've been going there. You show up consistently. You leave materials. You have a friendly working relationship with the front desk. When you leave, nobody does anything differently than they would if you hadn't come.

Knowing the People
You know individuals

You know specific parents by name, their dog's name, why they haven't committed yet or what made them sign up. A staff member texts you when a new eligible dog joins. A parent calls you because they ran into someone in the parking lot whose dog might be a fit. When you leave, someone there is still working for you.

The question isn't how many locations you visit. It's how many people in those locations know you well enough to think of you when you're not there.

What One Real Relationship Produces

Biscuit's owner called the recruiter on a Tuesday afternoon in October. Not because something was wrong. Not because she had a scheduled visit coming up. Because she ran into a new daycare member in the parking lot whose large dog was the right age and temperament, and she thought the recruiter would want to know.

What One Real Relationship Does
She called on a Tuesday. Nobody asked her to.

Biscuit's owner has been in the program for two years. The recruiter remembered her name on the second visit. Asked about Biscuit specifically. Followed up after a donation to check in. Made her feel like she was a person in the program, not a registration in a system.

That Tuesday call produced a sign-up within the week. The new dog is now a regular donor. The recruiter didn't visit Barkability that week. She didn't need to. One real relationship sent her the lead instead.

The Polite Brush-Off
Knowing When Enough Is Enough

Some locations give you the same vague answer every time. Renovations. Busy season. Get with so-and-so. They're not saying no — they're just not saying yes. And every visit you make to a location that isn't going anywhere is time you could spend deepening a relationship at a location that is.

The test: after multiple genuine attempts — showing up in person, talking to staff, leaving materials, asking specific follow-up questions — is there any signal that this location wants to move forward? Not a polite wave. An actual signal. A question from a staff member. A manager who remembers your name and brings it up. A parent who lingered.

When the answer is no, you have two choices: redirect your energy to a location that's giving you real signals, or make one more targeted attempt with a different approach before you do. What you don't do is keep visiting out of habit and call it persistence. Persistence that doesn't change its approach eventually becomes a cost the program can't afford.
