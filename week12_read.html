One at a Time:
The Only Thing That Works

Knowing a location and knowing the people in it are two completely different things. One of them builds a schedule. The other builds a program.

The Directive

Seven people on this team were asked this week why they do this job. Every single answer was personal. A dog that couldn't get blood. A family that almost lost their dog. A notification that said "we used your blood today." A retired career in veterinary medicine and a need to keep contributing. A closed greyhound colony and a hurricane and a moment of recognizing this is needed.

Not one person said "because it's a good program" or "because the metrics are compelling." Every answer went directly to a person, a moment, a dog, a decision. That's not a coincidence. That's the model.

Michael said it plainly: one relationship with every donor owner is the only thing that works. Seven years. Nothing else will work. He will not allow anyone to try something else. Because it doesn't work.

This week is about what that directive actually means when you're standing in a lobby.

A Patricia Moment
Patricia knows all of her locations. Patricia cannot name a single parent.

Patricia has seventeen locations on her schedule. She knows the layout of each one. She knows which staff member usually works the front desk on Tuesdays. She knows which locations have the most large dogs on Mondays. She has visited each location an average of six times.

She cannot name a single parent who would recognize her name if the daycare mentioned it. She cannot name a parent who would text her on a Thursday because they ran into a new daycare member whose dog might be eligible. She cannot name a parent who would tell someone else about the program without being asked.

Patricia considers this a territory challenge. Patricia is adding three more locations next month.

Knowing a Location vs. Knowing People
Knowing a Location
Familiar face at the front desk

You know the schedule. You know which days are busiest. The staff recognizes you when you walk in. You have a good relationship with the manager. You show up consistently. You know how many dogs are registered there. This is the foundation. It is not the relationship.

Knowing the People
Parents who call you

You know specific parents by name. They know your name. When something happens — a new eligible dog at the daycare, a question about the program, a friend whose dog might qualify — they think of you and contact you directly. That's the relationship. That's what produces donors who stay in the program, Murphys who close other parents, and cheerleaders who work for you when you're not there.

A parent who calls you on their own is worth more than ten sign-ups from parents who vaguely remember talking to someone at their daycare once.

How You Build It

It's not a technique. It's a decision to treat every conversation with a parent as the beginning of a relationship rather than an attempt to close a sign-up. The difference shows up in the questions you ask, the things you remember, and whether you follow up on a person rather than a location.

Tracy's instinct in her first week was exactly right — she wanted to reach out to every archived donor in Columbus personally, by name, to say the program is finally running and we're still interested in your dog. Not a mass email. Individual contact. That's the model at work even before she had a single active donor.

Hannah is doing it from her couch with a broken ankle. Every call she makes to a daycare, she's letting them know upfront that she'll be checking in every week — not because she's going to harass them, but because she wants them to expect her and trust that she's consistent. That consistency is the foundation of a relationship even when she can't be there in person.

What One Real Relationship Looks Like
Biscuit's owner calls. Not when something's wrong. On a Tuesday.

Biscuit's owner called the recruiter on a Tuesday afternoon in October. Not during a scheduled visit. Not because something was wrong. Because she ran into a new daycare member that morning whose dog was a large breed, and she thought of the program, and she thought of the recruiter, and she called.

That one call produced a conversation with the new daycare member, which produced a sign-up. The recruiter didn't generate that sign-up. The relationship generated it. The recruiter just had to answer the phone.

Patricia has seventeen locations. Patricia has never received a call like that. Patricia wonders why her numbers are not moving.

The Polite Brush-Off
Knowing When Enough Is Enough

There's a difference between persistence and presence. Persistence keeps showing up hoping something changes. Presence makes a real impression each time and reads the signals honestly.

When a location has given you the same vague answer for months — "we're renovating," "now isn't a good time," "let's revisit later" — and has never let you past the lobby, that's data. It's not a relationship in progress. It's a polite no that nobody wants to say out loud.

Michael said it directly: when the nuisance starts costing business it could go elsewhere, that's the signal. The question isn't whether you should give up on a location forever. It's whether continued visits at this frequency are the best use of the time, or whether that time produces more at a location where the door is actually open.

The recruiter who reads the brush-off accurately and redirects early has more time for the locations that are actually building. The recruiter who keeps visiting the same closed door six times a month has less time for everything else.

Bring This To Our Next Meeting
One parent by name — and what that relationship has produced

Not a manager. A parent. Someone who knows your name. Someone who would contact you unprompted if something came up that was relevant to the program. Share who it is, which location, and what that relationship has produced or what you're building toward. Patricia will not be sharing. Patricia knows many locations. Patricia knows no parents by name. Patricia is reviewing her territory strategy.
