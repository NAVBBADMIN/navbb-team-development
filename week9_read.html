How to Run a Challenge
That Both Locations Actually Feel

You are not the scorekeeper. You are the energy source. The competition is only as alive as you keep it.

The Concept

A competition between two partner locations is one of the highest-leverage tools available to a recruiter. Done well, it turns passive partners into active advocates, gets staff mentioning the program when you're not there, and gives you a legitimate reason to show up at both locations more often than you otherwise would.

Done poorly, it's a scorecard that nobody's looking at by week three.

The difference between the two isn't the competition design. It's the recruiter's presence during it. You launch it, yes. But the challenge only stays alive if you're feeding it — showing up at both locations, giving them updates on where they stand, naming that the other location is gaining ground. The FOMO doesn't maintain itself. You maintain it.

The Four Moments That Matter

A well-run partner challenge has four distinct moments — each one requires something specific from you. Missing any of them is what turns a challenge into a scorecard nobody looks at.

Moment 1
The Pitch

Not the same pitch to both. What gets Location A excited may not be what gets Location B engaged. Know which location has competitive instinct and lean into it. Know which one responds to relationship and community and use that angle. The pitch has to feel personal to each location — not like you're running the same program everywhere.

Moment 2
The Launch

Confirm the scoring clearly. Make sure the staff contact at each location can explain it in their own words before you leave. Answer every question on day one — if you say "I'll follow up on that," it creates doubt. The launch is also when you plant the first FOMO seed: let each location know the other one is in the program and already has momentum. That sentence alone changes the temperature of the room.

Moment 3
The Midpoint

This is where most challenges die. The recruiter checks in at the end but doesn't show up in the middle. The midpoint is the most important visit of the competition — it's when you tell each location where they stand, remind them there's still time to close the gap, and give the trailing location a specific reason to push. If you don't show up at the midpoint, the competition is already over in their minds.

Moment 4
The Close

Both locations should come out of the challenge with the relationship strengthened — not just the winner. The winning location gets recognition. The other location gets a clear picture of what moved the needle for the winner, and a reason to want to go again. The recruiter who closes well turns a one-time competition into a recurring dynamic that runs itself.

The challenge doesn't run itself. It runs because you show up at both locations and make both locations feel like they're in a real competition — not just on a list.

The FOMO Mechanic
Using Competitive Instinct at the Facility Level

Daycares know who their competitors are. The facility down the road that runs a similar operation is on their radar — and when they hear that location is doing something they're not, it matters to them.

When you launch the challenge, name it plainly: "The other location we're running this with has been in our program for a while and already has strong donor numbers. This is a chance to catch up — or pull ahead." That sentence does more work than any incentive structure. It gives the manager a reason to care that has nothing to do with prizes.

At the midpoint, use it again — specifically: "Location B picked up three new donors this week. You're still in this, but the gap is closing." The location that's ahead will push to protect their lead. The location that's behind will push to close it. You win either way because both locations are now working for you between your visits.

On the Scoring

Know this before you walk in to pitch either location. You should be able to explain it in under 60 seconds without looking at notes, and the staff member you're talking to should be able to repeat it back to you accurately before you leave.

2 points for each new dog that completes screening and is approved as an active donor. 1 point for each approved donor that shows up on a scheduled donation day — capped at once per dog per month, because dogs can only donate once per month. Only eligible donors count — dogs must be 50 lbs or more, ages 1 to 7, current on vaccines and preventatives, negative heartworm test in the last year, and never received a blood transfusion. No exceptions, because the moment a dog earns a point and then gets turned away at screening, you've lost the trust of that location.

If a staff member asks a question you don't have the answer to, answer it before you leave that visit. Don't say you'll follow up. Follow up means the question stays open and doubt grows into it.

Biscuit's Location
Biscuit's daycare manager started texting between visits. That's what a well-run challenge feels like.

Biscuit's daycare entered a challenge against a location two miles away. The manager had a friendly rivalry with that location already — they'd lost a couple of dogs to them over the years. When the recruiter mentioned at the midpoint that the other location had just added three new donors and was pulling ahead, the manager called her back that evening.

She wanted to know what she needed to do. She started mentioning the program to parents herself. She flagged three dogs that week she thought were eligible. She asked when the next donation event was so she could coordinate pickup timing to make it easier for owners to bring their dogs.

Biscuit's daycare won. The recruiter didn't make that happen by showing up more. She made it happen by making the manager feel the competition was real — and then getting out of the way while the manager ran with it.
