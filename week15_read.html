What You Are
Actually Asking For

A sign-up is a six-donation, twelve-month commitment backed by a real investment in that dog's health. The way you frame that determines whether the yes you get is a real one.

The Ask Is Bigger Than It Sounds

When a parent signs up their dog, they are not agreeing to drop by sometime. They are committing to a minimum of six donations over twelve months. The program is investing in their dog's health — bloodwork, parasite prevention, ongoing monitoring — and in return, the program needs consistent donors it can count on.

Most parents who ghost after sign-up are not bad people. They are people who did not fully understand what they were committing to. They said yes to something that felt light and easy in the lobby, and then when life got busy, that light and easy commitment was the first thing that slipped. A vague yes produces vague follow-through. A real yes — one where the parent understands what they are agreeing to and still says yes — produces a donor.

A Patricia Moment
Patricia does not mention the six-donation commitment.

Patricia does not want to scare people off. Patricia thinks if she mentions six donations up front, parents will say no. Patricia has a lot of sign-ups and a very low show rate. Patricia has never connected these two things. Patricia is designing a new flyer.

The parents who say no when they hear about the six-donation commitment are parents who were not going to show up anyway. Filtering them out at the lobby is not losing donors — it is saving Patricia a follow-up she was going to have to send eventually.

The Investment Frame

The single most effective reframe in the commitment conversation is this: stop talking about what you are asking the parent to give, and start talking about what the program is investing in their dog.

Screening costs hundreds of dollars. Parasite prevention medications are added on top. Ongoing health monitoring is built in. The program does all of this before a dog ever donates a single unit. That is a real investment — in that specific dog's health and longevity. The parent is not doing you a favor. You are doing them one. And in exchange, you are asking them to show up consistently so that investment can make the greatest possible impact.

We are investing in your dog's health. We want to make the greatest impact with that investment. We cannot do that if they only donate once.

This works on the owner who is already nodding and ready to sign up. It also works on the owner who is tuning out, already turning toward the door. It changes the framing from "can you do this thing for us" to "here is what we are doing for you, and here is what we are asking in return." That is a completely different conversation.

Three Things Every Commitment Conversation Needs
1

The investment frame

Before you ask them to sign up, tell them what the program is putting into their dog. Bloodwork. Parasite prevention. Health monitoring. Make the investment real and specific before you make the ask. Then the ask has something behind it.

2

The six-donation expectation, stated clearly

Not mentioned as a footnote. Not buried in the paperwork. Said out loud before they sign: "We ask for a minimum of six donations over twelve months." Then ask: "Does that work for you?" The parents who say yes to that are the ones who show up.

3

The specific day confirmed as workable

Tell them the collection day. Ask whether that day works for their schedule. If it does not, say so now while you can still have a real conversation about it. A sign-up where the parent cannot come on the collection day is not a sign-up — it is a name on a list.

The Qualifier Is the Gift

Confirming the day works and stating the six-donation commitment upfront feels like you are risking the sign-up. It is the opposite. You are filtering the list for the people who are actually going to show up. Every name you lose in the lobby because the day does not work or because six donations felt like too much is a name you were going to lose anyway — just three months later and after a lot more effort.

The parents who stay after hearing all three things are your donors. The ones who leave are doing you a favor by leaving now.
