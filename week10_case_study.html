Same Lobby, Same Dog, Two Conversations

~8 min · Click through each scene · One question at the end.
Scene 1 of 6
Thursday Afternoon — The Lobby

Same daycare. Same dog. Same owner. Two different conversations.

It's a Thursday afternoon at a mid-sized daycare. A golden retriever named Ranger just came out of the back — healthy, clearly well-cared-for, exactly the kind of dog the program is built for. His owner, a woman in her forties, is clipping on his leash while he wags at everything.

Two recruiters visit this same location on alternating weeks. Both will approach Ranger's owner. Both will describe the same program. One will get a registration. Watch what's different.

Patricia — The Information-Led Pitch

Patricia explains the program. Ranger's owner is interested.

Patricia approaches as Ranger settles down. She introduces herself and NAVBB. She explains the donation process, what a happy visit looks like, how healthy donors get a full exam included, and what the schedule looks like. She mentions that most dogs are totally relaxed by the second or third visit. She shows a photo of a donor dog looking comfortable and content at a donation event.

Ranger's owner

"Oh, that's really interesting. Ranger's pretty easygoing, I think he'd be great for that. Let me talk to my husband and look at our schedule."

Patricia gives her a flyer. The owner takes it. Ranger pulls toward the door. She tucks the flyer into her bag. Patricia marks her as a warm lead. Ranger's owner mentions it to her husband that evening. He says "sure, if you want to." She means to sign up. She forgets. Three weeks pass.

Recruiter B — The Urgency-Led Conversation

Recruiter B starts somewhere different.

Recruiter B approaches as Ranger is calming down. She introduces herself and then, before describing anything about the program, she says:

Recruiter B's opening

"Dogs like Ranger — healthy, his size, his temperament — are the reason other dogs survive emergencies. When a dog comes in needing a transfusion and we don't have enough blood, that's a dog that doesn't make it home. Ranger could change that."

Ranger's owner stops adjusting the leash. She looks up. That's a different kind of sentence than she was expecting. She asks what it involves. Recruiter B explains — but she doesn't lead with the happy visit. She leads with the outcome. The happy visit is mentioned as the experience, not the point.

The Moment That Matters

Recruiter B asks one question Patricia never thought to ask.

After explaining the program, Recruiter B pauses and asks:

The question

"Has Ranger ever been sick or had an injury where you were worried about him?"

Ranger's owner nods. She says he had a stomach obstruction two years ago. It was scary. The vet clinic didn't have blood on hand and had to call around. It delayed the surgery by two hours.

Recruiter B doesn't say anything for a second. Then: "That's exactly what we're trying to prevent for other families. Ranger is healthy now. He could be the dog someone else calls around for."

Ranger's owner signs up before she leaves the lobby.

What Was Different

Same program. Same dog. Same owner. Completely different outcome.

Patricia — Information-Led

Led with the experience: happy visits, comfortable process, included exam, easygoing dogs. Showed a happy dog photo. Owner was interested but not moved. Flyer went into the bag. Registration never happened. Patricia followed up by email two weeks later. No response.

Recruiter B — Urgency-Led

Led with the need: what happens when there isn't enough. Asked about the dog's history. Found the emotional entry point. Reflected it back. Owner connected her dog's story to someone else's emergency. Registered on the spot. No flyer needed.

The difference wasn't the information. It was the emotional entry point and the question that unlocked a personal connection to the urgency. Recruiter B didn't manipulate anyone. She helped Ranger's owner see something real — that her healthy dog exists in a world where other dogs desperately need what he has.

Your Turn

Think of the last conversation that ended with "I'll think about it." What was your opening — information or urgency?

Not the whole conversation — just the first 30 seconds. Did you start with what the program is and how it works? Or did you start with what happens when there isn't enough? What would have been different if you'd opened the other way?
