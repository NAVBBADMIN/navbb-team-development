The Location Isn't Going
to Change for You

Bad setup and bad location are not the same thing. One of them is a problem. The other is a puzzle — and puzzles have solutions.

The Concept

Every recruiter has at least one location that fights them. The lobby is the size of a closet. The dogs come out a side door and the parents barely stop the car. The owner is impossible to reach and you've been there six times without getting five minutes with her. The weather is the problem three out of every five visits.

Here's what separates the recruiters who figure these locations out from the ones who quietly stop showing up: they treat the setup as a constraint to solve, not a reason to lower their expectations. The location isn't going to redesign its lobby for you. The drive-thru pickup isn't going to become an indoor lobby experience. Your job is to find the version of this visit that works given what's actually there.

That's a skill. It gets easier with practice and with the discipline to document what you've tried instead of repeating the same approach and getting the same result.

A Patricia Moment
Patricia visited an outdoor drive-thru pickup location on a rainy Tuesday.

Patricia stood in the parking lot. Patricia got wet. The parents drove through, grabbed their dogs, and did not roll down their windows in the rain to talk to the person standing in the parking lot. Patricia spoke to zero people.

Patricia went home. Patricia's notes from the visit say "low turnout." Patricia is considering going back next Tuesday and doing the same thing. Patricia considers herself persistent.

Patricia has not yet considered moving her position to the covered area near the exit, getting there ten minutes before pickup starts, or asking the staff to flag eligible dogs before they come out. Patricia will consider all of this sometime in the future.

The Three Setup Types

Most non-ideal location setups fall into one of three categories. Each one has a different adaptation.

01
Outdoor / Drive-Thru Pickup

Parents drive up, dogs come out a side door, handoff happens in under 60 seconds. No lobby. No indoor conversation space. Weather-dependent every single visit.

The Adaptation

Position yourself before the pickup flow starts, not in the middle of it. Ask staff ahead of time to flag eligible dogs — know which ones you want to catch before they come out. A popup tent in the right spot can create a visible stopping point. The window is before the dog appears, not after.

02
Tiny Lobby / No Usable Space

The indoor space is the width of a hallway. No room to stand without blocking traffic. Staff has nowhere to put you that doesn't create a bottleneck.

The Adaptation

Take photos before you commit. Walk the space with the owner before scheduling a recruiting event. Know where you'd stand and what that looks like at peak pickup. A location with a tiny lobby can still produce — but you need to know the flow before you show up and discover the problem for the first time in front of an owner.

03
The Missing Decision-Maker

The owner isn't there. The staff can't help you. You keep emailing and calling and the one person you need is somehow never available when you show up.

The Adaptation

Pop in on an unscheduled day. Bring something small — pastries, a coffee. Make the visit about them, not about scheduling a recruiting event. For the "I need to ask my spouse" defer, ask permission to email them directly — give them a simple way to bring the conversation home without a flyer in the bottom of a bag.

Repeating the same approach at a difficult location and calling it persistence isn't persistence. It's the absence of a new idea.

The Information Advantage

One of the highest-leverage things a recruiter can do at any location — especially a difficult one — is get better information before walking in. Not general information about the location, but specific information about that day's dogs.

Biscuit's Story
The visit that worked because the recruiter knew who was coming.

At one location, a recruiter got access to the daycare's scheduling system. Before she arrived, she had a list of every dog expected that day — names, sizes, approximate ages. She cross-referenced it against dogs already in the program and arrived knowing exactly who she wanted to talk to and in what order.

She didn't waste time with dogs that were already in the pipeline. She didn't miss the window on a dog that left early because she found out it was there when it was already in the parking lot. She walked in with a plan and executed it. Biscuit was on that list. Biscuit's owner signed up that day because the recruiter knew her name before she walked in the door.

Not every location will share system access. But every recruiter can call ahead, ask about the day's dog count, ask which dogs are regulars, and build a rough picture before showing up. The ones who do this consistently will always outperform the ones who show up and see what's there.

On Social Proof in the Lobby

One thing that consistently breaks down parent hesitation is seeing that other dogs are already doing this. Parents who aren't sure want to know they're not the first — that other people have made this same decision and it went fine.

That's why knowing the program's history at a location matters as a recruiting tool: "We've been coming here for two years, we have twelve dogs from this daycare in the program, their owners all bring them back every month." That sentence does more work than a flyer. It tells the parent they're joining something that's already working, not trying something new.

The recruiter who can say that with confidence — because they know their location, know their donors, know the history — has an advantage that no pitch script can replicate.
