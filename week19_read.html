Three Lines You
Don't Cross Alone

The recruiter handles almost everything in the field. But there are three situations where the right move is not to answer — it is to hold the conversation and bring in the right person as fast as possible.

You Are the First Point of Contact. Not the Last.

The recruiter is the face of the program at every daycare. Parents know your name. Daycare managers call you first when they have a question. You are the relationship. And because of that, there is a real pull to handle things yourself — to answer the question, to make the judgment call, to keep the conversation moving.

Most of the time that instinct is right. But three situations require something different. Not avoidance, not silence — just the discipline to hold the conversation, share facts without opinions, and get the right person on the phone as fast as possible. That is not weakness. That is exactly what a professional does.

A Patricia Moment
Patricia had a question about kennel cough this week.

Patricia Googled it. Patricia found an article from 2019. Patricia shared what she found in the team chat with three bullet points about incubation periods. Patricia also shared it with the daycare manager who had asked the question. Patricia considered herself helpful. Patricia did not know that kennel cough is a syndrome caused by multiple pathogens with varying incubation periods that veterinary professionals spend careers understanding. Dr. Blake has since responded to the daycare manager. The conversation took 45 minutes to recalibrate.

The Three Lines
🔬
Line 1 — Medical Questions
Route to Dr. Blake or Dr. Galanis. Every time. No exceptions.

When a daycare owner, parent, or facility staff member asks you a medical question — about kennel cough, about pneumonia, about whether their dog can catch something from another dog during a donation — your job is not to answer it. Your job is to share facts and route the question.

What facts means: "Our veterinary team takes infectious disease very seriously and I want to make sure you get the most accurate information. Can I have Dr. Blake give you a call?" That is a fact. It is not an opinion. It does not accidentally give them something to focus on.

Why this matters: These conversations escalate fast. A daycare owner who hears something from you that does not match what their vet says later, or what they Google, or what Dr. Blake tells them, loses trust in the program. Dr. Blake said it directly: "They focus on what they want to focus on." Do not give them something wrong to focus on.

The same applies to kennel cough specifically: Kennel cough is a syndrome, not a single disease. Multiple pathogens. Varying incubation periods. It can look like a lot of things and none of those things are yours to explain. When a daycare reports illness — any illness — route it to the veterinary team immediately and do not speculate.

🟥
Line 2 — Aggression
Do not sign them up. You are the first line of defense for the collection team.

If a dog snaps at you during the meet-and-greet, or the daycare mentions bite history, or an owner uses the word unpredictable — that dog is not a candidate. Do not sign them up to see how they do at the screening. Do not note it and let the collection team find out on their own.

This is not a gray area. The collection team is putting hands on these dogs during a donation. A dog that is reactive in the lobby is a safety risk in the donation room. The recruiter who sees an aggression signal and flags it protects the team. The recruiter who lets it through hoping it will be fine has handed that problem to someone else at the worst possible moment.

What to say to the owner: "Our program requires dogs to be comfortable with handling from people they do not know well. I want to make sure we set up for success — I do not think this would be the right fit right now, but I appreciate you talking with me about it." Honest, kind, and clear.

💔
Line 3 — Compassion Calls
When a dog is permanently retired, the relationship is not over. It just changes.

When a dog receives a permanent retirement — cancer, a serious condition, a death — the recruiter's job is to make a call. Not an email. A phone call, to check in on the parent, to thank them for what their dog gave to the program, and to make sure they know about the benefit that most owners never hear about.

Every dog that has donated blood gets free blood for life. Three dogs have actually used this benefit. One family moved to Arizona and we FedExed blood overnight to them. This is something the parent should know — not as a selling point, but because it is true, it is meaningful, and it is something we can actually do for them.

Why it matters beyond the moment: Nine times out of ten, when a dog passes or becomes permanently ineligible, that owner gets another dog. The relationship you maintained through the difficult moment is the reason they call you when the new puppy turns one. Hannah put it simply: "I think keeping that connection even after a dog is medically ineligible permanently is good because it just shows the care."

Michael's words: "Just the simple fact of calling up an owner after their dog has passed is the type of place I like to work at. What do we get for it? Nothing. But it is the right thing to do."

Knowing when to step back is not a limitation. It is one of the most professional things a recruiter can do.

The Benefit Most Parents Never Hear About

Every dog that has been a blood donor gets free blood for life. This is not a hypothetical benefit — it has been used. A family moved to Arizona, their dog needed a transfusion, and we FedExed blood overnight. The dog got what it needed.

When you are in a difficult conversation with a parent whose dog is facing a health crisis, this is something you can offer them. Not as a pitch. Just as a fact: "If you ever find yourself in a situation where the dog needs blood, please reach out to us. That is something we can actually help with." Most parents have never been told this. For a parent going through something hard, it is the right thing to share.
