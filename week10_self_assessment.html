Question 1 of 6 · Emotional Framing
When I describe the program to a hesitant parent, I lead with the need — what happens to dogs who need blood when there isn't enough — before I describe what the experience is like for their dog.
Not at some point in the conversation — in the first thirty seconds.
Question 2 of 6 · Emotional Framing
I have a specific sentence I use to communicate the urgency of the program — not the program description, but why it matters right now.
A sentence that creates a feeling, not just an understanding. Something you could say and watch the person's expression change.
Question 3 of 6 · Urgency Communication
I've asked a hesitant parent about their own dog's health history — whether their dog has ever been sick or had a scare — as a way to make the urgency personal to them.
Not as a screening question. As an emotional entry point. Making the abstract real by connecting it to something they've already lived.
Question 4 of 6 · Urgency Communication
When I talk about the happy visit and the donation experience, I frame it as the means, not the message — after the urgency is established, not instead of it.
The happy visit is a feature. The lives it saves is the reason. Leading with the feature removes urgency even when it's accurate and true.
Question 5 of 6 · Cold Outreach Confidence
When a location hasn't responded to my emails or calls, I'm willing to show up unannounced rather than wait for a response that may not come.
Not as a last resort after months. As a deliberate tactic when the digital approach has clearly stalled.
Question 6 of 6 · Staff Relationships
I know the names of at least two staff members at each of my anchor locations — and they would recognize me if I walked in on a day I wasn't expected.
Not the manager only. Multiple people. So that staff turnover doesn't reset the relationship to zero.
